Abstract. State budgets reveal policy priorities, but states differ so much in size and fiscal capacity that absolute spending is a poor basis for comparison. This study asks whether unsupervised learning from an undergraduate syllabus can group Indian states and Union Territories by their budget priorities. We use a 31-unit cross-section (28 states and three Union Territories with legislatures) of 2025-26 Budget Estimates from the Reserve Bank of India’s State Finances: A Study of Budgets, described by five indicators: education and health as shares of aggregate expenditure, and development expenditure, social-sector expenditure and capital outlay as percentages of GSDP. After z-score scaling, K-means for k = 2–6 was compared by silhouette score, with Ward hierarchical clustering and PCA as companions. The highest silhouette (0.329) occurred at k = 5, but two of the five clusters are single units (NCT Delhi and Arunachal Pradesh), so the structure is better read as three broad groups plus two outliers. The first two principal components explained 86.8% of the variance. The three GSDP-scaled indicators are strongly correlated, so much of the clustering reflects the size of a state’s budget relative to its economy rather than the mix of spending. The data are a one-year budget snapshot and no link to development outcomes is claimed. A multi-year panel schema and reproducible code are provided.
Keywords: state budgets; fiscal priorities; RBI; K-means; hierarchical clustering; PCA; silhouette score; compositional data; SDG 16; SDG 17

1Introduction

State governments deliver most public education, health and local-infrastructure services in India, so their budgets are a direct record of policy priorities. Comparing those priorities is difficult because states differ greatly in population, income and revenue capacity; absolute rupee figures mostly reflect size. Shares and ratios are more comparable, provided the numerator and denominator are stated explicitly [1], [2].

Clustering fits this problem naturally because there is no label to predict: we want to discover which states resemble each other. This assignment applies the unsupervised methods of an undergraduate machine-learning syllabus – K-means, hierarchical clustering and principal component analysis (PCA) – to a cross-section of Indian state budgets. We ask: (RQ1) which fiscal profiles emerge from education, health and capital-related indicators; (RQ2) how many groups the data support; (RQ3) what drives the separation, as revealed by PCA and the correlation structure; and (RQ4) how the groups could be related to development outcomes without circular reasoning.

Our contributions are: (i) a documented feature table with an explicit denominator for every indicator; (ii) a leakage-free design in which development outcomes are kept out of the clustering and used only afterwards; (iii) one script that compares K-means solutions with three internal metrics and fits Ward clustering and PCA; (iv) a candid reading of the results, including outliers and redundant indicators; and (v) a multi-year panel schema for extending the work. The study supports SDG 16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions) by making public spending patterns easier to scrutinise, and SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals) by building on official open data and releasing reusable code.

Section 2 gives the background, Section 3 the data, Sections 4–5 the methods and set-up, Section 6 the results and Section 7 the discussion, followed by conclusions.

2Related Work and Background

The Reserve Bank of India’s annual State Finances report [1] draws on the budget documents of 31 state governments and Union Territories with legislatures, supplemented by CAG Finance Accounts where required, and publishes statements on deficits, expenditure composition and liabilities [2]. Statements 26 and 27 give education and health expenditure as shares of aggregate expenditure [3], [4]; Statement 35 gives select development-expenditure indicators [5]; and Appendix IV reports capital expenditure by state [6]. The report presents these as tables and narrative comparisons; this assignment adds a reproducible clustering workflow on top of them.

K-means [8] partitions observations into k groups by minimising the within-cluster sum of squared distances; it is fast but sensitive to scaling, outliers and initialisation. Ward’s method [9] builds a nested hierarchy by merging the pair of groups that least increases within-group variance. PCA [11] rotates correlated variables into orthogonal components ordered by explained variance; here it is used to visualise the clusters. The silhouette coefficient [10] compares cohesion with separation, and the Calinski–Harabasz [12] and Davies–Bouldin [13] indices are secondary internal measures. Fiscal shares can be compositional data [14] – non-negative parts of one whole – for which log-ratio transforms are appropriate; Section 3.3 explains why this does not hold for our five indicators. All models use scikit-learn [15]; general background is in [16].

3Dataset and Preprocessing

3.1  Source and description

The data come from the RBI publication State Finances: A Study of Budgets 2025-26, released on 23 January 2026 [1]. The analysis file has 31 observations: 28 states and three Union Territories with legislatures (NCT Delhi, Jammu and Kashmir, Puducherry), compiled from Statements 26, 27 and 35 [3]–[5]. All values are 2025-26 Budget Estimates (BE), i.e. planned, not audited, spending. Table 1 lists the five indicators.

Two points of vocabulary matter. Development expenditure and capital outlay are not the same as capital expenditure, and total capital disbursements can include debt repayment and other capital-account transactions. We therefore say capital outlay, and keep the distinction in the extended schema (Table 2). The “capital expenditure” priority of the problem statement is represented here by capital outlay relative to GSDP, not as a share of aggregate expenditure.

3.2  Cleaning and verification status

State names were harmonised across statements and the all-states aggregate row was removed. All 31 units have complete values for the five indicators, so no imputation was needed; the code drops, rather than imputes, any unit with a missing indicator. If a monetary field is ever used, RBI tables may report ₹ crore or ₹ lakh and must be converted to one unit first; this is not needed for the percentages used here. Verification status: the 31-unit table was compiled for the original analysis and has not yet been re-checked cell by cell against the RBI Excel files; this is a required step before submission (Section 7).

3.3  Scaling and the compositional-data issue

Each indicator is standardised using means and standard deviations over the 31 units, because the indicators have different ranges:

zij = (xij − μj) / σj(1)

Education and health are shares of aggregate expenditure, whereas the other three are percentages of GSDP. The five variables therefore share no common total and do not form a closed composition. The centred log-ratio (CLR) transform [14],

clr(x)j = ln xj − (1/D) Σl=1..D ln xl(2)

is valid only for strictly positive parts of a single whole, so applying it here would be inappropriate and we use z-scores. A proper CLR analysis needs mutually exclusive categories under one denominator (for example capital outlay, social services, economic services, interest payments and administration as shares of aggregate expenditure); the code and Table 2 prepare for this.

3.4  Relationships between the indicators

Fig. 1 shows the Pearson correlations. Education and health shares are moderately and positively correlated (about 0.6, read from the figure). The three GSDP-scaled indicators are very strongly correlated with one another (roughly 0.85–0.95), whereas education and health are only weakly related to them. In effect the five variables carry two main dimensions – the size of the budget relative to GSDP, and the weight of education and health – which anticipates the PCA result in Section 6.2.

RBI data include Union Territories with legislatures from 2017-18 onwards [2], which is a natural start for a consistent panel, and the e-STATES workbook [7] is a possible source. 4Methodology

Fig. 2 summarises the workflow. All steps after cleaning operate on the standardised matrix X with n = 31 rows and p = 5 columns.

Fig. 2. Methodology pipeline. Step 7 (dashed) needs external outcome data and is not part of the reported run.

4.1  K-means

K-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares

J = Σi=1..n ‖xi − μc(i)‖²(3)

where c(i) is the cluster of unit i and μc the centroid of cluster c. We fit k = 2,…,6 with random_state = 42 and n_init = 50 restarts and keep the k with the highest mean silhouette.

4.2  Ward hierarchical clustering

Starting from single units, Ward linkage repeatedly merges the pair of clusters A and B that least increases the within-cluster variance,

Δ(A, B) = [ |A||B| / (|A| + |B|) ] ‖μA − μB‖²(4)

The tree is cut at the same k as K-means so the two partitions can be compared with the adjusted Rand index (ARI).

4.3  PCA

PCA is fitted to X and the first two components are used only to visualise the clusters; clustering itself is done in the original standardised five-dimensional space.

4.4  Evaluation

For unit i the silhouette is

s(i) = [ b(i) − a(i) ] / max{ a(i), b(i) }(5)

where a(i) is the mean distance to the other units of its own cluster and b(i) the lowest mean distance to another cluster. Values near 1 mean well-separated clusters and values near 0 overlapping ones; in scikit-learn a single-member cluster has s = 0. The Calinski–Harabasz index (ratio of between- to within-cluster dispersion; higher is better) and the Davies–Bouldin index (mean similarity of each cluster to its nearest neighbour; lower is better) are computed as secondary diagnostics. No single metric is treated as proof that a given k is correct; cluster profiles, i.e. the means of the original indicators, support interpretation.

4.5  Linking clusters to development outcomes

Development outcomes (for example literacy, infant mortality, life expectancy or SDG-index scores) are deliberately excluded from the feature matrix. Once the clusters are fixed, outcomes can be joined by state and year and compared across clusters. Using outcomes to build the clusters and then comparing the same outcomes across them would be circular. Any such comparison is associational, not causal.

5Experimental Setup

Software. Python with pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn [15] and Matplotlib. The script run_experiments.py performs every step and writes all tables and figures to outputs/. Randomness. random_state = 42 and n_init = 50 are fixed. No train/test split. The task is descriptive and unsupervised, not predictive, so there is no held-out evaluation; with n = 31 the results are exploratory. Range of k. Candidates are k = 2–6; with 31 units larger k would produce very small clusters. For a panel, temporal validation should be added.

6Results

The figures and the cluster table in this section are those of the original analysis of the 31-unit extract. run_experiments.py regenerates them from the CSV (Appendix B).

6.1  Model selection

Fig. 3 shows the mean silhouette score for k = 2–6. The highest score, 0.329, is at k = 5; the other values lie between roughly 0.28 (k = 6) and 0.31 (k = 2 and k = 4), read from the figure. All scores are modest, so the data show only moderate cluster structure, not sharply distinct fiscal regimes. One caveat matters: at k = 5 two clusters contain a single unit each (Table 3), and a single-member cluster contributes a silhouette of zero. The gain at k = 5 is therefore probably due to setting the two most extreme units apart; this was not tested separately, but the script reports the number of single-member clusters for every k.

6.2  PCA view

PC1 explains 56.5% and PC2 30.3% of the variance, 86.8% together, so Fig. 4 is a faithful two-dimensional summary. NCT Delhi lies far to the upper left and Arunachal Pradesh far to the right, which is why they form single-unit clusters. From the cluster means (Table 3) and Fig. 1, PC1 appears to track the GSDP-scaled indicators – Arunachal Pradesh, with the highest development and capital-outlay ratios, has the largest PC1 – and PC2 the education and health shares, which are highest for NCT Delhi. This reading should be checked against the loadings that the script writes to outputs/pca_loadings.csv.

6.5  Development outcomes

The RBI fiscal statements contain no comparable outcome measures, and none were merged, so no relationship between clusters and development outcomes is claimed. The design of Section 4.5 can be applied by joining independent state-level indicators (for example literacy rate, infant mortality, life expectancy, per-capita NSDP or the NITI Aayog SDG India Index) to the cluster assignments by state and year. Because two clusters are single units and the others have 5 and 12 members, such a comparison should be descriptive (group medians, effect sizes) and should not lean on p-values from tiny groups.

7Discussion

What the results suggest. Five standardised indicators are enough to separate recurring fiscal profiles: a large group of states with low GSDP-scaled public spending, a group with about twice those ratios, a small group with high education shares, and two outliers. The workflow condenses 31 × 5 numbers into profiles that can be read and checked. The structure is, however, only moderate (silhouette 0.329) and dominated by one dimension: because the three GSDP-scaled indicators are strongly correlated, PC1 mostly measures how large state budgets are relative to GSDP and says less about how they are composed. Keeping only one of the three, or using ratios with a common denominator, is a natural sensitivity test.

Limitations. (i) The data are a one-year BE cross-section, not a panel, and BE may differ from Revised Estimates and Accounts. (ii) Two of the five clusters are single units, so the silhouette at k = 5 depends on outliers. (iii) The indicators have different denominators and three are largely redundant. (iv) Capital outlay/GSDP is not a capital-expenditure share of total expenditure, and development expenditure is not capital expenditure. (v) With n = 31, K-means is sensitive to outliers, scaling and initialisation, and small states and Union Territories have volatile ratios. (vi) No per-capita figures, debt stock, interest burden or outcomes are included. (vii) The feature table has not yet been independently re-checked against the RBI files. (viii) In a panel, repeated state-years are not independent; robustness checks should include clustering multi-year averages and tracking membership over time.

Policy relevance. Clusters can support peer comparison, showing which states have broadly similar fiscal structures and where capital emphasis or education and health weight differ. They must not be used as a league table: geography, demographics, revenue capacity, central transfers, committed expenditure and accounting practice all matter.

Ethics and SDG impact. Aggregate public budget data contain no personal information. The main risk is over-interpretation, since a cluster label can be mistaken for a verdict on a government; transparent definitions, shared code and stated limits address this. The project supports SDG 16, in particular target 16.6 (effective, accountable and transparent institutions), through comparable public-finance analysis, and SDG 17 through the reuse of official statistics and open code. The clustering itself does not measure institutional quality or partnership effectiveness.

8Conclusion and Future Work

Using 2025-26 Budget Estimates for 31 states and Union Territories, K-means, Ward clustering and PCA on five standardised fiscal indicators separate recurring budget profiles. The best silhouette score (0.329 at k = 5) indicates moderate separation and is partly due to two single-unit clusters, NCT Delhi and Arunachal Pradesh. The first two principal components explain 86.8% of the variance, and PC1 appears to reflect the GSDP-scaled indicators. The results are exploratory and descriptive, not causal.

Future work should (i) extend the data to a multi-year panel, with Accounts as the primary stage and BE/RE as sensitivity checks (Table 2); (ii) add the capital-expenditure share and the interest and committed-expenditure burdens with consistent denominators; (iii) run a CLR analysis on mutually exclusive expenditure categories; (iv) test robustness by dropping correlated indicators and clustering multi-year averages; and (v) join independent development outcomes after clustering, as in Section 4.5.

def resolve_columns(df):
    """Find the state column and map the CSV's columns onto the canonical feature names."""
    cols = list(df.columns)
    exact = [c for c in cols if c.lower().strip() in ("state", "state_ut", "state/ut", "state_name", "unit")]
    loose = [c for c in cols if "state" in c.lower()]
    state_col = (exact or loose or [cols[0]])[0]
    mapping = {}
    for canon, (_, _, keys) in FEATURES.items():
        if canon in cols:
            mapping[canon] = canon
            continue
        hits = [c for c in cols if c != state_col and any(k in c.lower() for k in keys)]
        if len(hits) != 1:
            sys.exit(f"Cannot map feature '{canon}' to a single CSV column (candidates: {hits}). "
                     f"Rename the column to '{canon}'. Columns found: {cols}")
        mapping[canon] = hits[0]
    if len(set(mapping.values())) != len(mapping):
        sys.exit(f"Two features map to the same column: {mapping}")
    return state_col, mapping

    df = df.dropna(subset=list(FEATURES)).reset_index(drop=True)
    if df["state"].duplicated().any():
        dup = df.loc[df["state"].duplicated(), "state"].tolist()
        sys.exit(f"Duplicate state rows {dup}. Use --year/--stage to select one observation per state.")
    print(f"Rows read: {n_raw}; aggregates removed: {n_raw - n_before}; "
          f"dropped for missing features: {n_before - len(df)}; used: {len(df)}")
    return df


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------- modelling
def model_selection(X, kmin, kmax, n_init):
    rows = []
    for k in range(kmin, min(kmax, len(X) - 1) + 1):
        km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, random_state=SEED, n_init=n_init).fit(X)
        sizes = np.bincount(km.labels_)
        rows.append({
            "k": k,
            "silhouette": silhouette_score(X, km.labels_),
            "calinski_harabasz": calinski_harabasz_score(X, km.labels_),
            "davies_bouldin": davies_bouldin_score(X, km.labels_),
            "inertia": km.inertia_,
            "smallest_cluster": int(sizes.min()),
            "n_singleton_clusters": int((sizes == 1).sum()),
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


def link_outcomes(assign, path, outdir):
    """Post-hoc only: outcomes are never used to build clusters."""
    oc = pd.read_csv(path)
    key = lambda s: s.astype(str).str.lower().str.replace(r"[^a-z0-9]", "", regex=True)
    scol = "state" if "state" in oc.columns else oc.columns[0]
    left = assign[["state", "cluster"]].assign(_k=key(assign["state"]))
    right = oc.assign(_k=key(oc[scol])).drop(columns=[scol])
    merged = left.merge(right, on="_k", how="left").drop(columns="_k")
    num = [c for c in merged.select_dtypes("number").columns if c != "cluster"]
    means = merged.groupby("cluster")[num].mean().round(3)
    means.insert(0, "n_units", merged.groupby("cluster").size())
    means.to_csv(os.path.join(outdir, "outcomes_by_cluster.csv"))
    tests = []
    for c in num:
        groups = [g.dropna().values for _, g in merged.groupby("cluster")[c]]
        groups = [g for g in groups if len(g)]
        try:
            p = kruskal(*groups).pvalue if len(groups) > 1 else np.nan
        except ValueError:
            p = np.nan
        tests.append({"outcome": c, "n_matched": int(merged[c].notna().sum()), "kruskal_p": p})
    pd.DataFrame(tests).to_csv(os.path.join(outdir, "outcomes_tests.csv"), index=False)
    print("Outcome linkage written (descriptive; small clusters, no causal claim).")

def main():
    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__, formatter_class=argparse.RawDescriptionHelpFormatter)
    ap.add_argument("--data", required=True, help="CSV with one row per state/UT")
    ap.add_argument("--out", default="outputs")
    ap.add_argument("--kmin", type=int, default=2)
    ap.add_argument("--kmax", type=int, default=6)
    ap.add_argument("--k", type=int, default=None, help="override the silhouette-selected k")
    ap.add_argument("--n-init", type=int, default=50)
    ap.add_argument("--year", default=None)
    ap.add_argument("--stage", default=None, help="e.g. Accounts, RE or BE (never mix stages)")
    ap.add_argument("--outcomes", default=None, help="optional CSV of development outcomes (post-hoc profiling)")
    a = ap.parse_args()
    os.makedirs(a.out, exist_ok=True)

    df = load_data(a.data, a.year, a.stage)
    feats = list(FEATURES)
    shorts = [FEATURES[f][0] for f in feats]
    X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[feats])

    sel = model_selection(X, a.kmin, a.kmax, a.n_init)
    sel.to_csv(os.path.join(a.out, "model_selection.csv"), index=False)
    best_k = a.k or int(sel.loc[sel["silhouette"].idxmax(), "k"])
    print("\nModel selection (K-means):\n", sel.round(3).to_string(index=False))
    print(f"Selected k = {best_k}")

    km = KMeans(n_clusters=best_k, random_state=SEED, n_init=a.n_init).fit(X)
    labels = km.labels_ + 1
    ward = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=best_k, linkage="ward").fit_predict(X) + 1
    ari = adjusted_rand_score(labels, ward)

    assign = df.assign(kmeans_cluster=labels, ward_cluster=ward,
                       silhouette_i=silhouette_samples(X, labels))
    assign.rename(columns={"kmeans_cluster": "cluster"}).to_csv(os.path.join(a.out, "state_cluster_assignments.csv"), index=False)
    pd.crosstab(assign.kmeans_cluster, assign.ward_cluster, margins=True).to_csv(os.path.join(a.out, "hierarchical_vs_kmeans.csv"))
    pd.DataFrame([{"adjusted_rand_index": ari}]).to_csv(os.path.join(a.out, "hierarchical_agreement.csv"), index=False)

    prof = df.assign(cluster=labels).groupby("cluster")[feats].mean().round(2)
    prof.insert(0, "n_units", pd.Series(labels).value_counts().sort_index())
    prof.to_csv(os.path.join(a.out, "cluster_profiles.csv"))
    (df.assign(cluster=labels).groupby("cluster")["state"].apply(lambda s: "; ".join(sorted(s)))
       .to_csv(os.path.join(a.out, "cluster_members.csv")))
    df[feats].corr().round(3).to_csv(os.path.join(a.out, "correlation_matrix.csv"))

    pca_full = PCA().fit(X)
    pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit(X)
    pcs = pca.transform(X)
    pd.DataFrame({"state": df.state, "PC1": pcs[:, 0], "PC2": pcs[:, 1], "cluster": labels}).to_csv(
        os.path.join(a.out, "pca_coordinates.csv"), index=False)
    pd.DataFrame(pca_full.components_[:2].T, index=feats, columns=["PC1_loading", "PC2_loading"]).round(3).to_csv(
        os.path.join(a.out, "pca_loadings.csv"))
    pd.DataFrame({"PC": range(1, len(feats) + 1), "explained_variance_ratio": pca_full.explained_variance_ratio_}).to_csv(
        os.path.join(a.out, "pca_explained_variance.csv"), index=False)

    make_figures(df, feats, shorts, X, sel, best_k, labels, pcs, pca, a.out)
    if a.outcomes:
        link_outcomes(assign, a.outcomes, a.out)

    info = {"n_units": int(len(df)), "features": feats, "selected_k": best_k,
            "silhouette_at_k": float(sel.loc[sel.k == best_k, "silhouette"].iloc[0]),
            "cluster_sizes": {int(k): int(v) for k, v in pd.Series(labels).value_counts().sort_index().items()},
            "adjusted_rand_ward_vs_kmeans": float(ari),
            "pc1_pc2_variance": [float(v) for v in pca.explained_variance_ratio_],
            "seed": SEED, "n_init": a.n_init,
            "python": platform.python_version(), "scikit_learn": sklearn.__version__,
            "pandas": pd.__version__, "numpy": np.__version__}
    with open(os.path.join(a.out, "run_info.json"), "w") as fh:
        json.dump(info, fh, indent=2)
    print("\nCluster profiles:\n", prof.to_string())
    print(f"\nWard vs K-means adjusted Rand index: {ari:.3f}")
    print(f"PC1+PC2 variance: {100 * pca.explained_variance_ratio_.sum():.1f}%")
    print(f"All outputs written to {a.out}/")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
